01
Structure · The Cosmic Web
A web without edges
Scientific fact
The universe is threaded by a colossal hidden framework called the cosmic web. Invisible dark matter dominates the scaffolding, while galaxies and gas trace its shape like luminous foam on invisible ocean waves. The web has three key ingredients: nodes, which are rich clusters of galaxies; filaments, the long strands of matter connecting the nodes; and voids, enormous bubble-like regions where almost nothing resides. This network is not static—gravity keeps drawing matter along the filaments into the nodes, making the web a dynamic, ever-tightening tapestry that stretches across billions of light-years.
The universe is not a scatter of isolated islands, but a web with no border.
We stand at one node, mistaking it for the whole — when it is only one crossing among countless connections.
Every filament is taut. Every void breathes.
02
Structure · Topological forms
Cluster, loop, void
Scientific fact
In the mathematical language of topology, the cosmic web can be decomposed into three primitive shapes. Blobs (connected components) are isolated clumps of matter—like scattered pearls. Loops (tunnels or cycles) represent the filamentary bridges and ring-like structures. Voids (cavities or shells) are the nearly empty chambers enclosed by walls of galaxies. Topology doesn't care about size or exact shape; it cares about how these pieces are connected. It turns the messy distribution of matter into a clean alphabet: clustered matter, through-connected matter, and empty chamber.
Three forms: gather, connect, empty. Gathered into stars, connected into filaments, emptied into shells.
A void is not an absence — it is another way of existing.
A loop is not a closure — it is a tunnel to somewhere else.

Structure · Multi-scale
A nested universe
Scientific fact
Cosmic structure doesn't exist on just one scale—it is deeply hierarchical. Tiny satellite galaxies live inside larger galactic halos, which are strung along filaments, which themselves are woven into great walls, and those walls curve around the largest voids. This nesting is fractal-like, though it has an upper cutoff. Topological analysis captures exactly this nesting: small blobs are embedded in larger loops, which in turn surround enormous cavities that contain yet smaller structures inside their walls. The universe is a set of structures within structures, all the way up and down.
Inside the void there are walls. On the walls, filaments. On the filaments, clusters. Inside the clusters, smaller voids.
The universe is a set of Russian dolls — no innermost, no outermost.
03
Persistence · Betti curves
The heartbeat of the universe
Scientific fact
Picture scanning the universe with a density threshold, like lowering the water level to reveal submerged features. At very high density, only the brightest, densest blobs appear. As the threshold drops, bridges and filaments emerge, and eventually vast voids open up. The counts of blobs, loops, and voids rise and fall as the threshold changes, tracing wavy lines called Betti curves. These curves look remarkably like an electrocardiogram—a rhythmic pulse of appearance and disappearance. A cosmic heartbeat written not in sound, but in the rise and fall of connected shapes.
The heartbeat of the universe is not a sound — it is an alternation of shapes.
Each time the density drops, a generation of clusters dies and a generation of voids is born.
A Betti curve is not mathematics — it is the universe counting the holes in itself.

Particles · Neutrinos
The invisible authors
Scientific fact
Neutrinos are the lightest massive particles we know, with a mass at least a million times smaller than an electron's. They zip through entire planets as if they were empty space. Yet their sheer abundance—trillions pass through your body every second—gives them cosmological leverage. Because they move so fast, they smooth out matter on small scales, erasing tiny clumps that would otherwise form. This “free-streaming” effect imprints itself onto the entire topology of the cosmic web, turning neutrinos into invisible sculptors of the large-scale universe.
They are so light they barely have weight. Yet numerous enough to change the shape of everything.
Invisible particles, in invisible places, rewriting all that can be seen.
Neutrinos are not the protagonists — but they wrote the script the protagonists follow.
05
Particles · Voids as information
The secret lives in the empty
Scientific fact
Surprisingly, the most sensitive probes of the cosmos's hidden contents may not be the crowded clusters of galaxies, but the empty voids. Because voids are so quiet—their internal gravity is gentle, their matter sparse—they respond exquisitely to the faintest cosmic players, such as massive neutrinos. The shape, size, and distribution of voids carry a clean imprint of physics that gets washed out in denser regions. If you want to weigh a ghost-like particle, the best place to look is where almost nothing exists.
You spent a lifetime staring at the stars — but the answer was hiding in the space between them.
A void is not nothing. It is where the universe keeps its secrets.
The largest structure is the quietest one.

Particles · Suppression effect
Voids broken into pieces
Scientific fact
A heavier neutrino carries more momentum, smoothing out matter on larger scales. This makes the cosmic web less crunchy. Large, pristine voids—those deep, undisturbed basins of emptiness—get broken into smaller, shallower voids. The void population shifts: the number of large voids decreases while smaller void-like pockets proliferate. The whole void distribution shrinks toward the average density, as if a heavy blanket were pressing down on the bubbly foam of the universe, squashing the biggest bubbles into a froth of many tiny ones.
Heavy particles crowd the voids. What was once vast open space gets cut into pieces.
The voids did not disappear — they became fragmented, shallower, no longer alone.
13
Particles · Matter fields
Skeleton and flesh
Scientific fact
In cosmological models, the “cold dark matter field” includes only the heavy, slow-moving dark matter that clumps easily. The “total matter field” also includes the light, fast-moving neutrinos. When scientists analyse the topological features of these two fields, they find that the total matter field is much more sensitive to neutrino mass. The difference does not arise because neutrinos create special isolated patterns; it's a proportion effect. Because neutrinos contribute a fixed fraction of the total mass, their presence dilutes the clustering power of the heavier dark matter in a uniform way, reshaping the entire topology like a subtle adjustment of the map's scale bar.
The dark matter field is a map that records only the skeleton. The total matter field is the flesh.
Neutrinos take up no space — yet their presence changes the scale of the entire map.

Observation · Redshift distortion
The crooked universe
Scientific fact
We can't stretch a tape measure across the cosmos. Instead, we use redshift—the stretching of light due to cosmic expansion—to estimate distances. But galaxies also have their own motions, and those peculiar velocities distort the map. Galaxies speeding toward us appear slightly closer, while those racing away seem farther. The result is a warped picture: dense clusters get stretched along our line of sight like taffy, while empty voids get squeezed. This distortion isn't a mistake; it's a signature of gravity and motion that, when decoded, reveals the true underlying structure.
You thought you were looking at the universe. You were looking at its reflection — stretched by velocity, flattened by direction.
In redshift space, everything is crooked. But inside the crooked lies the true.
